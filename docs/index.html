This project analyzes the Gender Wage Gap between 2013-2016.

Introduction

While the gender wage gap is well known, it doesn’t affect all occupations equally. Some industries show larger differences than others. This raises an important question — what factors actually drive these wage differences? Specifically, this project asks whether occupation gender composition, workforce size, and industry category are associated with differences in the gender wage gap across occupations. Understanding this can help us move beyond just identifying the gap to explaining it. Our goal is to look at occupation-level variation in the gender wage gap to understand what factors drive differences in earnings. The data comes from TidyTuesday, which sourced the dataset from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The data was collected from 2013 to 2016 and has about 2,000 observations that focus on earnings, percent female, and industry. For methods, we use linear regression to analyze relationships, Lasso and Ridge to identify important predictors, and K-means to explore patterns beyond industry categories.

Exploring the Data

One of our outcome variables, which we created is wage_gap, calculated as the dollar difference between male and female median earnings. This variable captures the raw income disparity and allows us to examine the magnitude of the earnings gap. We expect this value to be generally positive, indicating men have a higher median earnings across most occupations.

A second outcome variable we are using, wage_percent_of_male, is included in our dataset. It measures female median earnings as a percentage of male median earnings. A value of 100 would indicate perfect parity; values below 100 indicate women earn less than men. This variable allows us to analyze relative disparity. We expect this variable to generally fall below 100 for most occupations.

Our key explanatory variables are: -percent_female: The percent of females for specific occupation -total_workers: Total estimated full-time workers > 16 years old -major_category: Broad category of occupation -minor_category: Fine category of occupation -occupation: Specific job/career -year: years from 2013-2016

We chose those variables based on both economic reasoning and our EDA. Percent female captures gender composition, which is central to our question, while total workers and industry categories help control for structural differences across occupations. The inclusion of year also allows us to examine whether patterns in the wage gap changed over time.

Data Wrangling and Transformation

We saw a considerable amount of missing values in the wage_percent_of_male column of our dataset. The author of TidyTuesday noted this is because those occupations have small sample sizes in total_workers, workers_male, and workers_female. The wage_percent_of_male metric calculated from those values may be unrepresentative and was thus reported as NA. To address this, we conducted two separate analyses. In one version, we drop all observation with NA wage_percent_of_male values, and filtered out all observation of that same occupation across other years for better reliability and consistency for our time-series analysis. We called this case our complete case. In the other, we retained these observations, as occupations with small workforce sizes or extreme gender composition may actually capture insightful patterns. For this set of analysis, we recomputed the NA wage_percent_of_male values using the total_earnings_male and total_earnings_female columns. We called this case our imputed case. In terms of data transformation, we created a new variable: wage_gap. It measures the dollar difference between the male and female median earnings (male minus female). This variable captures the magnitude of the wage disparity in absolute terms and complements the percentage-based measure.

Methodology

To answer our question, we used k-means clustering, multiple linear regression, and lasso and ridge modeling. We used the complete cases data set, as seen in the eda the complete cases data set proved to be more stable, so to ensure accuracy we used teh complete cases data set in our modeling.

Multiple Linear Regression We begin by using multiple linear regression to analyze how occupational characteristics relate to female earnings as a percentage of male earnings. The results show that percent female has a positive and statistically significant relationship with wage parity, suggesting that occupations with higher percentages of female workers tend to have slightly higher female earnings relative to male earnings. Several industry categories also show meaningful differences in wage parity, while total workers is not statistically significant, as you can see in model 1.. Overall, the model suggests that occupation composition and industry structure are associated with variation in the gender wage gap across occupations.

Lasso and Ridge Regression We then used Lasso and Ridge regression to address multicollinearity and identify important predictors. An 80/20 train-test split was implemented to predict female earnings as a percentage of male earnings using percent female, total workers, industry, and year as predictors. Cross-validation was used to select the penalty parameter, and model performance was evaluated using R-squared. The results show that percent female remained an important predictor in both models and was not shrunk toward zero, indicating its continued significance. Lasso did not eliminate any variables, suggesting that multiple factors contribute to the gender wage gap rather than a single dominant driver. Ridge regression produced a similar pattern, and both models yielded comparable R-squared values. Even with regularization, the models did not explain substantially more variation, implying that important explanatory variables may be missing from the dataset.

K-MEANS Finally, we used K-means clustering to explore patterns in the data beyond predefined industry categories. The clusters show groupings based on percent female and female earnings as a percentage of male earnings. For example, one cluster contains more female-dominated occupations with relatively higher wage parity, while another captures more male-dominated occupations with lower wage parity outcomes. A third cluster reflects occupations with lower wage parity across a wider range of gender compositions. This suggests that wage patterns are shaped not only by industry, but also by broader structural characteristics within occupations. As shown in Figure 7, each cluster contains a mix of major occupation categories, suggesting that the original industry classifications are not strong standalone identifiers of wage gap patterns. The K-means model was based on two variables because adding additional variables made the clusters more difficult to interpret and visualize clearly.

Overall, across all methods, we find a consistent relationship between gender composition and wage outcomes, highlighting that the wage gap varies systematically across occupations.

Results:

The analysis shows that the gender wage gap persisted across occupations throughout 2013–2016, with female earnings generally remaining below male earnings across most industries. Descriptive analysis found that female earnings averaged roughly 84% of male earnings, indicating a consistent wage disparity within the dataset.

The multiple linear regression results suggest that occupational gender composition is significantly associated with wage parity. Occupations with higher percentages of female workers tended to have slightly higher female earnings relative to male earnings. However, the relationship varied across industries, and several occupation categories displayed meaningful differences in wage parity outcomes. The relatively low R-squared values suggest that additional unobserved factors likely contribute to variation in the wage gap. Percent female remained statistically significant across the regression models and was also retained in both regularized models, suggesting that occupational gender composition is an important predictor of wage parity. In contrast, total workers was not statistically significant in the multiple linear regression and had relatively little influence in the regularized models. Several industry categories also showed meaningful differences in wage parity outcomes.

The Lasso and Ridge regression models produced similar findings. Percent female remained one of the strongest predictors in both regularized models, while several industry-category coefficients were shrunk close to zero. This suggests that occupational gender composition plays an important role in explaining wage parity, although multiple variables contribute to wage differences across occupations. The Lasso model identified percent female as one of the strongest retained predictors of wage parity. In contrast, total workers was shrunk very close to zero, suggesting limited predictive importance. The year variable was effectively removed from the model, indicating that time effects contributed relatively little explanatory power compared to occupation characteristics and industry structure. Ridge regression produced similar overall predictive performance.

K-means clustering further showed that industry alone does not fully explain wage patterns. The clustering results identified occupation groupings based on gender composition and wage parity rather than strictly by predefined industry categories. Each cluster contained a mix of major occupation sectors, suggesting that broader structural characteristics within occupations influence wage outcomes across the labor market.

Discussion

This analysis has several limitations. First, the dataset only covers the years 2013–2016, meaning the results may not fully reflect more recent labor market trends or changes in wage inequality over time. In addition, the dataset is highly aggregated at the occupation level, which limits the ability to account for important individual-level factors such as education, experience, hours worked, geographic location, and firm-specific differences.

There are also methodological limitations to consider. The regression models identify correlations rather than causal relationships, meaning we cannot conclude that changes in occupational gender composition directly cause changes in the wage gap. Additionally, the relatively low R-squared values suggest that other important explanatory variables are missing from the analysis. The K-means clustering also showed overlap across clusters, indicating that occupation groupings are not perfectly distinct and that wage patterns are influenced by multiple interacting factors.

Future research could improve this analysis by using a longer time range and incorporating more detailed individual-level data. Additional research could also examine how occupational segregation, education, and labor market structure contribute to wage disparities beyond gender composition alone.
